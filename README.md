# Workshop_Philosophy
An HTML slider tool to help which shop organization philosophy works best for you.
Shop System Levers: Layers
A small, single-file HTML tool for exploring how different workshop-storage and organization philosophies might fit your shop. Instead of treating the whole workshop as one system, it lets you tune the approach for different layers—from heavy machines to small consumables.

The tool is intended to help you think through tradeoffs and find a setup that suits your work. Its philosophy presets are illustrative starting points, not official or definitive representations of any person’s or organization’s methods.

Use it
Save the HTML as index.html.
Open it in a modern web browser.
Select a layer, then adjust its sliders or load a preset.
Review the match and tension indicators as you explore different settings.
No installation, server, or external dependencies are required. The tool runs in the browser.

Workshop layers
Each layer can have its own settings:

Machines & layout — large, heavy items that move rarely.
Station tools — daily-use tools associated with a task or workstation.
Carry & job kits — carts, trays, and bags that move around the shop or to jobs.
Precision & rare — fragile or seldom-used tools and equipment.
Hardware & consumables — small items that are numerous and frequently replenished.
The “Your shop at a glance” summary shows the closest preset match for every layer, so you can see where your approach differs across the shop.

The seven levers
Move each slider between two ends of a design choice:

Rigid ↔ Flexible — stable locations and muscle memory, or room to reconfigure.
Fixed ↔ Mobile — permanent placement, or equipment that can move with the work.
Central ↔ Point of use — one shared home, or tools stored where they’re used.
Enclosed ↔ Open — protection from dust and bumps, or visible contents and missing items.
Standard module ↔ Custom fit — interchangeable storage, or holders tailored to particular tools.
Habit ↔ Protocol — informal routines, or written rules and audits.
Spacious ↔ Dense — easier access and more working room, or greater storage capacity in less space.
The end labels describe the tradeoff, not a “right” answer. A different setting may make sense for each layer.

Presets
Presets load example slider settings into the currently selected layer. They are starting points you can adjust, not complete shop plans.

The included presets are Paulk-ish, Savage-ish, Sachs-ish, Toyota 5S-ish, NASA-ish, and Tool chest-ish. The “-ish” naming signals that the settings are simplified interpretations for this tool, not endorsements or authoritative summaries of those approaches.

How matching works
For the selected layer, the tool compares your seven slider values with the values assigned to each preset. It calculates a similarity score and displays the closest match or, when scores are close, a blend. If no preset is close enough, it reports “No close match: your own system.”

These percentages are relative similarity scores—not a measure of workshop quality, productivity, safety, or how faithfully you follow a philosophy.

Tensions
The tension cards flag combinations of settings that can work against one another—for example, combining mobile storage with tools kept at a fixed point of use. The percentage indicates how strongly the selected settings trigger that particular tension in the tool’s simple model.

A flagged tension isn’t necessarily a problem. It’s a prompt to consider a tradeoff, such as making a duplicate tool, using a kit, or changing how a station is organized.

Design principles
The tool is built around a few practical organizing goals:

Every item has a home.
The home is visible to the system.
Storage uses standard units or modules where useful.
Retrieval takes few steps.
Putting items away is easy.
Missing items are obvious.
Notes
Changes are held in the page while it remains open; there is no save or export feature.
The page supports light and dark appearance, following the browser or device color scheme.
The presets, scores, and tensions are heuristic examples intended to support reflection—not a scientific assessment or a substitute for shop-specific judgment.
